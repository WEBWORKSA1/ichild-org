Sleep · 5–12 years

Healthy sleep for school-age kids: routines that work

By the iChild.org Editorial Team · Reviewed against public pediatric guidance · Updated 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

How much sleep children aged 6–12 need, how to build a calm bedtime routine, and solutions for common sleep struggles.

How much sleep do kids need?

Children aged 6–12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep in 24 hours; teens need 8 to 10. Many school-age children get less, which can show up as irritability, trouble focusing, hyperactivity and more frequent illness — sometimes mistaken for behavior problems.

Build a predictable routine

  1. Fixed wake-up time, even on weekends (within an hour). This anchors the body clock.
  2. Count back from wake-up to set bedtime — use our bedtime calculator.
  3. Wind down for 30–45 minutes: bath or shower, pajamas, teeth, a book, a short chat.
  4. Screens off an hour before bed, and devices charge outside the bedroom.
  5. Keep it the same every night — predictability is the point.

Set up the bedroom for sleep

  • Cool, dark and quiet. Blackout curtains help in summer.
  • A dim nightlight is fine if your child is afraid of the dark.
  • Use the bed for sleep and reading — not homework or gaming.

Daytime habits that help nighttime

  • At least an hour of physical activity and time outdoors in daylight.
  • No caffeine (including many sodas and iced teas).
  • A light snack is fine; avoid heavy meals right before bed.

Common struggles

Bedtime stalling: build "one more" into the routine (one extra hug, one sip of water), then hold the line kindly. A visual checklist helps.

Worries at bedtime: try a "worry time" earlier in the evening to talk things through, or a worry journal.

Night waking or nightmares: comfort briefly and calmly, then return them to bed.

Talk to your doctor about loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing, frequent night terrors, or daytime sleepiness despite enough sleep — these can be signs of a treatable sleep disorder.

Important: This guide is general education, not medical advice. Every child is different — talk with your pediatrician, family doctor or child-health nurse about your child's specific needs.
