Feeding · 4–12 months

Starting solid foods: signs of readiness, first foods and allergy tips

By the iChild.org Editorial Team · Reviewed against public pediatric guidance · Updated 2026-11-18 · 6 min read

When and how to start solids, purées vs. baby-led weaning, how to introduce common allergens, and choking safety.

When to start

Most babies are ready for solids around 6 months — not before 4 months. Look for readiness signs rather than a date:

  • Sits up with little or no support and holds head steady
  • Shows interest in food — watching you eat, reaching for food
  • Opens mouth when food approaches and can move food to the back of the mouth rather than pushing it out with the tongue

Breast milk or formula remains the main source of nutrition until the first birthday. Solids add iron, new textures and skills.

What to offer first

Iron-rich foods are a priority, since babies' iron stores fall around 6 months: iron-fortified cereals, puréed or soft meats, lentils, beans, tofu and eggs. Then add a rainbow of vegetables and fruits. There is no need to start with vegetables before fruit, or to wait days between every new food (except when you want to track a reaction).

Purées or baby-led weaning?

Both work. Spoon-feeding purées lets you offer iron-rich foods easily; baby-led weaning (soft finger foods from the start) builds self-feeding skills. Many families mix the two. What matters most: soft textures that squish between your fingers, progressing to lumpier and finger foods by about 8–9 months.

Introducing allergens early

Current guidance supports introducing common allergens — peanut (as smooth peanut butter thinned or powder, never whole nuts), egg, dairy, wheat, soy, fish and sesame — around 6 months, once baby has started other solids, and then keeping them in the diet regularly. Babies with severe eczema or a known egg allergy may need medical advice first.

Foods to avoid in the first year

  • Honey (risk of infant botulism)
  • Cow's milk as a main drink (small amounts in cooking are fine)
  • Added salt and sugar, juice
  • Whole nuts, whole grapes, popcorn, hard raw vegetables and other choking hazards

Choking safety

Always seat your baby upright and supervised. Cut round foods (grapes, cherry tomatoes) into quarters lengthwise; cook hard vegetables until soft. Gagging is a normal reflex as babies learn — choking is silent. Consider an infant first-aid course. Read our picky eating guide for the toddler years ahead.

Important: This guide is general education, not medical advice. Every child is different — talk with your pediatrician, family doctor or child-health nurse about your child's specific needs.
