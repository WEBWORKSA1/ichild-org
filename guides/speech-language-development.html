Development · 0–5 years

Speech and language development: what to expect and how to help

By the iChild.org Editorial Team · Reviewed against public pediatric guidance · Updated 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Typical speech and language milestones from babbling to storytelling, everyday ways to boost talking, and when to ask about a speech evaluation.

Speech vs. language

Language is understanding and using words and gestures to share ideas. Speech is the physical production of sounds. A child can understand a lot (receptive language) before saying much (expressive language).

Typical milestones

AgeMost children…
2–4 monthsCoo, turn toward voices, make sounds back when you talk
6–9 monthsBabble with consonants ("ba-ba", "ma-ma"), respond to their name
12 monthsUse gestures like waving and pointing, say a special word for a parent, understand "no"
18 monthsSay several words, follow one-step directions, point to show you things
2 yearsCombine two words ("more juice"), point to pictures in books when named
3 yearsHold short conversations, ask "what/where/why" questions, are understood by family most of the time
4–5 yearsUse sentences of four or more words, tell simple stories, are understood by most people

Everyday ways to boost language

  • Talk all day: narrate what you're doing and what they're looking at.
  • Wait: pause after you speak, giving your child time to respond — even with a coo or gesture.
  • Expand: if they say "car", you say "Yes, a red car going fast!"
  • Read and sing daily: rhymes and songs highlight the sounds of language.
  • Offer choices: "Apple or banana?" invites a word instead of a nod.
  • Reduce background TV, which cuts down on conversation.

Bilingual families

Raising children with two or more languages does not cause speech delay. Speak the language(s) you're most comfortable with — rich language in any language helps.

When to ask for an evaluation

Talk to your doctor or request a speech-language assessment if your child isn't babbling by 12 months, isn't using single words by about 16–18 months, isn't combining words by 2, is hard to understand by 3, stutters with tension or frustration, or loses words or skills. Hearing should be checked as part of any evaluation. Track progress with our milestone checker.

Important: This guide is general education, not medical advice. Every child is different — talk with your pediatrician, family doctor or child-health nurse about your child's specific needs.
