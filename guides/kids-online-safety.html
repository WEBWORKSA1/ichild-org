Digital life · 6–12 years

Online safety for kids: a parent's starter kit

By the iChild.org Editorial Team · Reviewed against public pediatric guidance · Updated 2026-11-18 · 6 min read

Practical settings, conversations and family rules to help children use games, apps and the internet safely.

Start with connection, not just controls

Parental controls help, but the most protective factor is a child who will tell you when something feels wrong. Stay curious about their online world: ask them to show you their favorite game or video, and keep conversations calm so they're not afraid of losing their device.

Set up devices safely

  • Use built-in parental controls on phones, tablets, consoles and streaming services.
  • Turn on safe search and child profiles; disable in-app purchases or require approval.
  • Set games and apps to friends-only chat, or turn chat off for younger children.
  • Keep devices in shared spaces for younger kids, and out of bedrooms overnight.
  • Check age ratings — most social media platforms require users to be at least 13.

Teach the core rules

  1. Keep personal info private: full name, school, address, phone, passwords and location.
  2. Strangers online are strangers: never meet someone from the internet without a parent.
  3. Trust the "uh-oh" feeling: if something makes you uncomfortable, stop and tell a trusted adult. You won't be in trouble.
  4. Think before posting: photos and messages can be copied and shared forever.
  5. Be kind online: don't share anything you wouldn't say face to face.

Know the warning signs

Secretiveness about devices, new "friends" you don't know, gifts or money from unknown sources, upset after being online, or sudden withdrawal can signal cyberbullying or grooming. Stay calm, save evidence (screenshots), block and report the account, and contact the platform and, if needed, local authorities or national child-protection hotlines.

Create a family tech agreement

Write down when, where and how devices are used, what happens when rules are broken, and how the agreement changes as your child grows. Let children help write it — they're more likely to follow it. Pair it with our screen-time planner.

Important: This guide is general education, not medical advice. Every child is different — talk with your pediatrician, family doctor or child-health nurse about your child's specific needs.
