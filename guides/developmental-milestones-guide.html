Development · 0–5 years

Developmental milestones from birth to 5: the complete parent guide

By the iChild.org Editorial Team · Reviewed against public pediatric guidance · Updated 2026-11-18 · 7 min read

What milestones are, what most children do at each age, how to support every stage, and the signs that mean it's time to talk to your doctor.

What is a developmental milestone?

A milestone is a skill that most children can do by a certain age — smiling, babbling, walking, saying two-word phrases, hopping on one foot. Health agencies set milestone checklists at the age by which about three out of four children show the skill. That means a missed milestone isn't a diagnosis; it's a signal to look a little closer.

Milestones are usually grouped into four areas that develop together:

  • Social and emotional — smiling at people, showing affection, playing with others, coping with feelings.
  • Language and communication — cooing, babbling, gestures like pointing, words, then sentences and stories.
  • Cognitive (thinking and problem-solving) — exploring objects, finding hidden toys, pretend play, counting and sorting.
  • Movement and physical — head control, rolling, sitting, crawling, walking, running, drawing and buttoning.

The big picture, year by year

Birth to 6 months: connection

Babies learn that the world responds to them. Expect social smiles by around 2 months, laughing and cooing back and forth by 4–6 months, steady head control and rolling. Every time you answer a coo, you're building the brain's wiring for language.

6 to 12 months: exploration

Sitting, reaching, transferring toys hand to hand, babbling strings like "bababa", stranger shyness and, by the first birthday, waving, pulling to stand and saying a special name for a parent. Object permanence appears — babies look for toys you've hidden.

12 to 24 months: independence

Walking, climbing, pointing to show interest, following simple directions, copying chores and an explosion of words. By 2, most children combine two words ("more milk") and point to body parts.

2 to 3 years: imagination

Pretend play takes off. Sentences lengthen, "why" questions arrive, and children begin to play alongside — and then with — other children. Running, jumping and kicking a ball become routine.

3 to 5 years: readiness

Conversations, storytelling, counting, naming colors, taking turns in games, drawing shapes and early letters, hopping and catching. These skills form the foundation of school readiness.

How to support development every day

  • Talk, read and sing constantly — narrate routines and name what your child is looking at.
  • Follow their lead in play; let them choose and you extend.
  • Offer tummy time and floor time for babies, and plenty of active outdoor play for toddlers and preschoolers.
  • Limit screens, especially before 18 months, and co-view when you use them.
  • Keep routines predictable so children feel secure enough to explore.

When to talk to your doctor

Speak up at any well-child visit — or call sooner — if your child:

  • Is missing several milestones for their age
  • Loses skills they once had (at any age)
  • Doesn't respond to sounds or their name, or rarely makes eye contact
  • Isn't babbling by 12 months, using single words by about 16–18 months, or two-word phrases by 2 years
  • Shows strong preference for one side of the body

Ask for a developmental screening. Many countries recommend routine screening at 9, 18 and 30 months, and autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months. Early intervention services are most effective when started early — and you can usually request an evaluation yourself.
